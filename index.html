THE REAL SHAPE OF MATTER

Electrons aren't dots
on a track.

An electron doesn't circle the nucleus like a planet — it exists as a smeared-out wave, a cloud of probability with a genuine 3D shape. That shape depends on the orbital: 1s is a sphere, 2p is a dumbbell, 3d branches into four lobes. The cloud above is drawn from that real math, not from an artist's guess.

99.9%Empty Space

Remove the empty space from every atom in your body and all that's left would fit in a sugar cube.

10⁻¹⁵mNucleus Diameter

The nucleus is about 100,000 times smaller than the atom it sits inside.

1926Year the Cloud Won

Schrödinger's wave equation replaced Bohr's fixed orbits with probability — the model this page renders.

HOW TO READ AN ORBITAL

Four numbers describe
every electron.

Every cloud in the simulator is one solution of the Schrödinger equation, and each solution is labelled by four quantum numbers. Learn to read them and the periodic table stops being a list to memorise.

n

Principal · size & energy

n = 1, 2, 3 and up. The larger n is, the farther out the cloud sits and the more energy the electron has.

l

Angular · shape

l runs from 0 to n − 1. l = 0 is an s orbital, 1 is p, 2 is d, 3 is f.

mₗ

Magnetic · direction

Which way the orbital points. There are 2l + 1 choices: one s, three p, five d, seven f.

mₛ

Spin · up or down

Every electron has spin +½ or −½. Two electrons can share an orbital only if their spins are opposite.

RULE 1

Aufbau · lowest first

Electrons fill the lowest-energy orbitals first: 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d and on. A few elements, like chromium and copper, bend the order.

RULE 2

Pauli · two per orbital

No two electrons share all four quantum numbers. That caps each orbital at two electrons, and each shell n at 2n²: 2, 8, 18, 32.

RULE 3

Hund · spread out first

Within a subshell, electrons occupy separate orbitals with parallel spins before they pair up, like passengers taking empty bus seats.

    HOW SMALL IS SMALL

    Eight orders of magnitude,
    atom to quark.

    Each step down in this scale is ten times smaller than the last. From the edge of the atom to the smallest thing anyone has measured, you cross eight of them.

    10⁻¹⁰ m

    The atom

    About ten million atoms fit across one millimetre. Nearly all of an atom's volume is the electron cloud.

    10⁻¹⁴ m

    The nucleus

    About 100,000 times smaller. Scale the atom to a 100 m stadium and the nucleus is a grain of sand at the centre, holding over 99.9% of the mass.

    10⁻¹⁵ m

    Proton & neutron

    Each is roughly 1.7 femtometres across, and is itself a swarm of quarks and gluons.

    <10⁻¹⁸ m

    Quark & electron

    No experiment has found any size for them. Their radius is below 10⁻¹⁸ m, so far as anyone can tell they are points.

    Masses are in MeV/c². A free neutron is unstable: it decays in about 15 minutes. Inside a nucleus it can be stable.

    LIGHTPhoton mode: what a photon really is

    Light is not a stream of tiny bullets, and it is not only a wave. A photon is a packet of the electromagnetic field whose arrival at a detector follows probability. Photon mode shows that directly, with thirteen scenes, each with live numbers such as wavelength, energy in eV, and momentum.

    Yin-yang cloud

    Photon energy drawn as particles. One half is the electric field, the other the magnetic field, twisting with spin.

    Linear wave

    A transverse wave: E and B in phase, perpendicular to each other and to the direction of travel.

    Circular wave

    The field rotates once per wavelength, tracing a helix. Each photon carries spin angular momentum of ±ħ.

    Wave packets

    Gaussian, sech², atomic and dispersive-glass pulses side by side.

    Real photon

    A photon is never a tiny ball. A detector absorbs it at one random point, and many clicks rebuild the beam spot.

    OAM vortex

    Twisted light: Laguerre–Gauss beams with a helicoid phase front and intertwined arms.

    Atom emission

    The photon an excited atom releases radiates like an oscillating dipole: a doughnut pattern with nothing along the axis.

    Atomic photon

    Spontaneous emission: a sharp leading edge and an exponential tail.

    Gaussian pulse

    Fourier-limited, no dispersion, so the shape never changes in vacuum.

    Sech² pulse

    The envelope of a mode-locked laser or a fibre soliton.

    Pulse in glass

    Dispersion and chirp in fused silica: red leads, blue lags, and the pulse broadens as it travels.

    SPDC rings

    One pump photon splits in a crystal into two. Two interlocking rings form, as in long-exposure photographs.

    Bell pair

    An entangled yin-yang pair with polariser angles you can set, and the CHSH test beside it.

    Light is a wave of two fields

    Electric field E and magnetic field B oscillate in phase, at right angles to each other and to the direction of travel. This is the linear wave scene in Photon mode.

    Spin: the field that corkscrews

    In circular polarisation the field rotates once per wavelength and traces a helix. Each photon carries spin angular momentum of ±ħ.

    ENTANGLEMENTTwo particles, one shared state

    The idea in plain words

    Two entangled particles share one quantum state. Measure one, and the outcomes you get from the other are correlated more strongly than any classical rule could explain, no matter how far apart they are. It does not send a message faster than light, but it is real, tested, and the basis of quantum cryptography and quantum computing. The 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics honoured the experiments that proved it.

    What Entangle mode shows. A pump photon splits inside a BBO crystal into a signal and an idler photon, drawn as the two interlocking rings seen in long-exposure photographs. Where the rings cross, a photon cannot be labelled horizontal or vertical, so each pair is polarisation-entangled. The Bell-pair scene lets you set the polariser angles and reads out the correlations, the probabilities P(++), P(+−) and the CHSH value, where classical physics caps the result at 2 and quantum mechanics reaches up to 2√2.

    One photon in, an entangled pair out

    A pump photon splits in a crystal into a signal and an idler. Horizontal photons leave on one cone, vertical on the other. Where the rings cross, a photon cannot be labelled H or V, so each pair is polarisation-entangled.

    Feel the entanglement

    For a Φ⁺ pair, the match between the two results depends only on the angle between the polarisers. Drag it and watch the probabilities change instantly.

    QUESTIONS

    Good questions,
    short answers.

    Why a cloud instead of orbits?

    Quantum mechanics describes an electron with a wave function. Squaring it gives the probability of finding the electron at each point, so the cloud is denser where the electron is more likely to be. There is no path to draw, only odds.

    Is this what an atom really looks like?

    No picture is literal, because atoms are far smaller than the wavelength of visible light. The clouds are computed from the orbital mathematics, the same shapes chemistry textbooks show, and drawn as points of light so you can see their form.

    What does Real Image Mode do?

    It switches to a photographic-style rendering and lets you choose how the cloud is coloured: Ember, Brand, Warm, Red, Classic, Radial, Angular or Shell.

    What does Antimatter mode show?

    It swaps the scene to a mirror palette. In the Nucleus Explorer, protons and neutrons are relabelled as antiprotons and antineutrons.

    Are the electron configurations correct?

    They are ground-state configurations using the Madelung filling order, with the known exceptions (chromium, copper, palladium, gold and several lanthanides and actinides) applied. For the heaviest elements they are predictions, since those atoms exist for only fractions of a second.

    Does it run on a phone?

    Yes. On small screens the panels become a bottom sheet, and the cloud density is lowered by default so it stays smooth. You can raise it in the Cloud & Zoom controls.

    Can I share a link to one element?

    Yes. Add the symbol to the address: simulation.html#el=Fe opens the simulator on iron, and the Nucleus Explorer accepts the same form.

    Is it free?

    Yes. It runs in the browser with nothing to install.

    CONTRIBUTE

    Everyone was taught the atom.

    THE PICTURE WE ALL CARRYA century-old drawing is still in our heads

    Close your eyes and picture an atom. A nucleus in the middle, tiny electrons circling it like planets. Almost everyone sees the same thing, because almost everyone was taught the same thing. That model is from 1913. It is useful, and it is wrong about the one thing chemistry depends on: shape.

    The real atom is a cloud of probability. A 1s orbital is a sphere, a 2p is a dumbbell, a 3d branches into lobes. Why bonds form, why metals conduct, why gold is yellow: it all follows from those shapes. Yet most people only ever meet them as flat ink on a textbook page, memorise them for an exam, and quietly decide that quantum physics is not for them.

    That is the real loss. Not a wrong diagram, but millions of curious people who walked away from physics because nobody let them look at it.
